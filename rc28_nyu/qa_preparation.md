# Expert Q&A: Questions from Occupational Mobility Scholars

## Purpose

This file anticipates questions from scholars who study occupational
mobility, networks, job structure, social stratification, and quantitative
methods. The questions are informed especially by the published research
agendas of Siwei Cheng and Xi Song. They are informed predictions, not claims
about what either scholar will actually ask.

Do not memorize every answer. Memorize the bold first sentence and one
supporting point.

# I. Questions from a Relational and Network Perspective

## 1. In mobility research, an edge represents an observed worker flow. What
exactly is the edge in your analysis?

**Our source-target relation is an analytical risk-set relation, not an
observed interaction or worker flow.**

The target is the occupation that changes. The source is an occupation that
already holds the same skill and therefore locates the target relationally.
Our evidence shows that change is ordered by the signed status gap between
them. It does not show that the source directly transmitted the skill.

## 2. If you do not observe transmission, why use the word diffusion?

**We use diffusion to describe a directional change in the distribution of
skill requirements across occupational profiles.**

Diffusion here is a population-level pattern. Current holders define where a
skill is already located, and we ask where adoption and abandonment occur
relative to those positions. I would describe the result as directional
diffusion, but not direct pairwise transmission.

## 3. Why expand one target-skill change into many source-target-skill rows?

**The triadic design preserves information that a target-only model would
average away.**

For the same target and skill, some current holders are above the target and
others are below it. The expanded risk set allows us to estimate how change is
associated with each relative position. A target-level model using the average
status of current holders would be a useful descriptive complement, but it
would collapse the relational heterogeneity that the main model studies.

## 4. Does repeating the same target-skill outcome across sources create
pseudo-replication?

**That is a real feature of the risk set, and we address it through weighting
and clustered inference.**

We give each target-skill opportunity equal total weight by using the inverse
number of eligible sources. The directional conclusions remain and the
magnitudes change little. We also cluster inference across the shared source,
target, and skill dimensions.

## 5. Why treat every current holder as a potential source? Many occupations
may never interact.

**The broad risk set avoids imposing an unobserved interaction network.**

We account for plausibility using task-profile distance: distant
occupational pairs are separated from close pairs. But we still do not know
which pairs interact organizationally. Restricting the risk set using observed
worker mobility, job-posting overlap, or organizational links would be a
valuable extension.

## 6. Why use task-profile similarity instead of an observed worker-mobility
network?

**The two measures answer different questions.**

Profile similarity captures whether occupational content is related before
the focal change. A mobility network captures where workers actually move and
may reveal additional boundaries. Connecting the two would allow us to test
whether worker flows facilitate skill recomposition, but that is beyond the
current occupation-skill design.

## 7. Could discrete occupational boundaries matter more than a continuous
status gap?

**Yes. The current paper tests a status gradient, not every possible
occupational boundary.**

Task-profile distance captures one horizontal dimension, while the signed gap
captures vertical position. A natural extension is to test whether diffusion
changes when a source-target pair crosses an empirically observed mobility or
skill-community boundary.

## 8. Are source-target relations stable when occupational profiles are
themselves changing?

**We anchor the relational structure at baseline to preserve temporal
ordering.**

Baseline holders, status, and profile distance define the opportunity
structure before subsequent adoption or abandonment. This prevents the
outcome from redefining its own predictors. The cost is that we do not model
the co-evolution of occupational relations during the interval.

## 9. Is Panel B really relational evidence, or just another fixed-effect
specification?

**Panel B is relational because it holds the changing occupation and the skill
fixed while varying the source's position.**

The target is the same and the skill is the same. Only the location of current
holders above or below that target changes. The same domain reversal therefore
cannot be explained only by a general tendency of high-status targets to
change more.

## 10. Could your results help explain increasingly rigid mobility
boundaries?

**They suggest a possible content-based pathway, but we do not test worker
mobility directly.**

If cognitive content accumulates in higher-status occupations while physical
content accumulates below, the skill distance between mobility destinations
may widen. That could reinforce mobility boundaries. Establishing that link
requires joining occupational-content change to observed worker transitions.

# II. Questions from Occupational Restructuring and Mobility Research

## 11. Why freeze occupational status in 2015 if occupational status itself
changes over time?

**We freeze status because the question is whether later skill change follows
the preexisting hierarchy.**

A time-varying status measure would partly incorporate the same changes we are
trying to explain. Baseline status gives temporal ordering. It does not imply
that occupational status is permanently fixed; modeling the co-evolution of
status and skill content is an important next step.

## 12. Why reduce occupational status to one dimension when occupations are
multidimensional?

**The vertical index and the horizontal profile distance play different
roles.**

The status index summarizes wage, education, and cognitive content along a
common hierarchy. Profile distance retains horizontal differences in
occupational content. We also use each status component separately and remove
cognitive content from the index. The directional conclusion does not depend
on one particular version of status.

## 13. How do occupational coding changes and newly created occupations affect
your result?

**We prioritize comparability by analyzing harmonized occupations observed at
both endpoints.**

This reduces the risk that a coding revision appears as skill change, but it
also means that the paper is about recomposition among continuing occupational
categories. It is not a study of the birth or disappearance of occupations.

## 14. Could the pattern simply reflect economy-wide changes in the prevalence
of skills?

**Skill fixed effects absorb stable differences in prevalence, and the RCA
checks address changes in the relative denominator.**

We freeze the economy-wide RCA denominator at its 2015 value and also examine
raw O*NET importance change. The central domain-specific directions remain.
This makes a purely compositional explanation less plausible.

## 15. Are lower-status occupations actually losing cognitive content in
absolute terms?

**Not necessarily. Our central result is relative and directional, not a claim
of universal absolute decline.**

Lower-status occupations may add some cognitive requirements. The result is
that adoption and retention are more strongly oriented toward higher-status
occupations relative to current holders. Broad upgrading and widening
hierarchical differences can occur at the same time.

## 16. How do you separate structural change from relational sorting?

**The fixed effects absorb general propensities to change, while the signed
gap captures position within a source-target comparison.**

Panel A holds source and skill fixed. Panel B holds target and skill fixed.
Their agreement shows that the pattern is not only a change in the marginal
distribution of occupations or skills. We still describe it as a relational
association, not a causal decomposition.

## 17. Could the result be driven by the pandemic or by the early AI period?

**No single disruption period reproduces the full domain-specific pattern.**

We compare 2015-2018, 2019-2021, and 2022-2024. Magnitudes vary, but the
overall reversal is not confined to the pandemic or early generative-AI
period. A common-shock permutation check provides an additional test.

## 18. What does this imply for workers' careers?

**The worker-level implication is plausible but not observed in this paper.**

Changing occupational content may alter the skills workers can carry to other
jobs and the destinations available to them. But we do not observe individual
workers or transitions. Linking our measures to longitudinal mobility data is
necessary before making a worker-level claim.

## 19. Could these changing occupational characteristics be transmitted
across generations?

**That is a direct motivation for a future project, not a result of the current
paper.**

If children enter occupational environments similar to those of their
parents, they may inherit not only occupational status but also exposure to
replaceable or protected forms of work content. Studying that possibility
requires parent-child occupation data combined with historical measures of
occupational content and technological exposure.

## 20. Would a time-varying occupational rank change your conclusions?

**It would answer a different and complementary question.**

Our baseline rank asks whether content change follows an existing hierarchy.
A time-varying rank would allow us to study whether changes in content and
changes in occupational standing move together. That is a valuable extension,
but it would not replace the baseline design.

# III. Questions Both Perspectives Could Raise

## 21. Does a Matthew effect require repeated observations of cumulative
advantage? You mainly compare endpoints.

**We identify a Matthew-effect pattern, not a complete longitudinal mechanism
of cumulative advantage.**

Across the interval, adoption and abandonment both reinforce the baseline
hierarchy. That is consistent with cumulative advantage because gains and
retention align in the same direction. With two endpoints, however, we cannot
observe every intermediate sequence or claim a self-reinforcing causal loop.

## 22. Is Matthew effect different from occupational polarization?

**Polarization describes the distribution; Matthew effect describes the
reinforcing pattern of change.**

Our contribution is to show that gains and losses are systematically aligned
with the existing hierarchy. The phrase Matthew-effect pattern emphasizes
that occupational recomposition reproduces an initial advantage rather than
simply documenting a cross-sectional divide.

## 23. For abandonment, why call the other occupation a source if nothing is
transmitted from it?

**For abandonment, source means positional referent rather than transmitter.**

The target sheds a skill while the source continues to locate that skill in
the occupational structure. This allows us to ask whether shedding occurs
above or below occupations that retain the requirement. We should not describe
that as a directly observed flow from one occupation to another.

## 24. Is status causally directing diffusion?

**No. Status direction is an estimated association, not an identified causal
effect of status.**

The fixed effects and robustness checks rule out several compositional and
measurement explanations. They do not randomly assign status or identify the
organizational mechanism. Our strongest claim concerns the structure and
direction of the pattern.

## 25. Why is cognitive content part of status if cognitive skills are also an
outcome?

**Status is measured at baseline, and we directly test the circularity
concern.**

We reconstruct the status index without cognitive content and also use wage
and education separately. The central directions remain. Cognitive content is
therefore not mechanically generating the result, although it is substantively
part of the occupational hierarchy.

## 26. What would be the best next test of your argument?

**The best next test would connect occupational skill change to observed
organizations and worker mobility.**

Identified job postings could reveal which firms alter requirements and when.
Resume or administrative data could show whether worker flows connect the
occupations involved. That would test whether the relational opportunities in
this paper correspond to actual organizational and career pathways.

# IV. Questions Suggested by Han and Cheng's Recent AJS Paper

## 27. Are skill type and skill diversity the same thing?

**No. Our paper studies which kinds of skills move across the occupational
hierarchy; Han and Cheng study how diverse the bundle of skills within a job
becomes.**

An occupation can acquire more sensory-physical content and simultaneously
develop a more diverse skill bundle. Likewise, acquiring socio-cognitive
content does not automatically mean that a job becomes more diverse or more
complex. These are complementary dimensions of occupational change.

## 28. Does occupation-level O*NET data hide variation among jobs within the
same occupation?

**Yes. O*NET identifies changes in the average occupational profile, but it
cannot show whether every job within that occupation changes in the same
way.**

Han and Cheng show that within-job and between-job skill diversity are
empirically distinct. Our analysis therefore identifies change at the
occupation level. Identified job postings would be needed to determine which
firms and which jobs within an occupation are driving that change.

## 29. Han and Cheng find rapid skill diversification in low-wage service
jobs. Does that contradict your Matthew-effect interpretation?

**No, because diversification is not equivalent to upward movement in the
status hierarchy.**

Their result shows that low-wage service jobs may absorb increasingly broad
and unusual skill combinations, often without comparable economic returns.
Our result concerns the domain and status direction of adopted and retained
content. Together, the studies suggest that low-status work can become more
demanding while the recognition and valuation of that content remain unequal.

## 30. Why use fixed skill domains when relationships among skills can change
over time?

**Fixed domains make the direction of change comparable over time, but they do
not capture a changing topology among skills.**

Han and Cheng estimate a relational skill space from co-occurrence patterns.
Our classification instead asks whether a requirement belongs to a general
socio-cognitive, specialized socio-cognitive, or sensory-physical domain. A
dynamic embedding would be a valuable extension for testing whether the
meaning and proximity of skills themselves are changing.

## 31. Could the result reflect unequal valuation rather than unequal skill
acquisition?

**Unequal valuation is a plausible mechanism, but our current design does not
separate valuation from task allocation or adoption capacity.**

Han and Cheng show that similar increases in skill diversity receive very
different earnings returns across occupational groups. That evidence makes a
valuation mechanism credible. Our paper establishes where content changes;
it does not identify why the same content is differently rewarded.

## 32. Could gender and racial composition explain the occupational-status
pattern?

**The current model does not identify demographic heterogeneity within
occupations.**

This matters because diversification in low-wage service work is especially
relevant to occupations with high shares of women and racial minorities.
Occupation-level composition could be examined as a moderator, but job- or
worker-level data would be needed for a stronger account of who absorbs new
requirements and who receives the returns.

## 33. Do O*NET and job postings measure the same thing?

**No. They capture related but distinct aspects of work content.**

O*NET provides structured occupation-level profiles of work requirements.
Job postings describe the employer's stated demand at the point of hiring and
may omit assumed skills or include aspirational requirements. Agreement across
the sources would strengthen the inference, but disagreement would also be
substantively informative rather than automatically invalidating either
measure.

## 34. Could task delegation explain the downward movement of physical or
service content?

**Yes. Task delegation and offloading are mechanisms compatible with the
observed direction, but they are not directly observed.**

Han and Cheng describe how precarious service workers may absorb tasks shifted
from more protected professional roles. That mechanism fits part of our
interpretation, although our pattern also includes other possible processes,
such as differences in credentials, organizational capacity, and valuation.

# V. Questions Suggested by Song et al.'s Recent Science Advances Paper

## 35. How is content recomposition different from occupational growth and
decline?

**They are different margins of labor-market restructuring.**

Song and colleagues study changes in occupational size and the mobility of
workers between growing, stable, and declining occupations. We study changes
in the requirements attached to continuing occupations. An occupation can
remain stable in size while its content changes, or grow while its internal
content is recomposed.

## 36. Do your source-target relations correspond to observed worker
transitions?

**Not in the current paper. Our relations describe an occupational skill
opportunity structure, not observed worker mobility.**

Song and colleagues observe origin-destination transitions in CPS data. A
direct next test would ask whether the source-target positions identified in
our paper predict those observed transitions or the accessibility of growing
occupations.

## 37. Where are the opportunity constraints in your model?

**Occupational mass enters the gravity formulation, but we do not observe each
worker's feasible destination set.**

Song and colleagues explicitly weight destination alternatives by employment
size or projected vacancies. Our occupational-level design handles exposure
and source multiplicity, but it cannot represent individual constraints such
as geography, credentials, firm access, or actual vacancies.

## 38. Would the conclusion change if occupations were weighted by their
number of workers?

**That would change the estimand from an occupational pattern to a
worker-weighted labor-market pattern.**

Our main question gives occupational skill opportunities analytical weight.
Event weighting also prevents a target-skill change from contributing more
simply because it has many eligible sources. A worker-weighted analysis would
be an important complement, especially for assessing the population-level
consequences of the pattern.

## 39. Could net occupational change hide substantial turnover within an
occupation?

**Yes, just as stable occupational size can conceal inflows and outflows,
stable average content can conceal job-level recomposition.**

Our adoption and abandonment measures reveal two margins of occupation-level
content change, but O*NET averages may still hide simultaneous changes among
jobs within the same occupation. This is another reason to connect the study
to identified job postings.

## 40. Could a linear signed status gap hide a nonlinear or polarized pattern?

**Yes. The current presentation summarizes the dominant signed gradient, not
every possible nonlinear form.**

Song and colleagues show that growing and declining occupations can both have
more mobility than stable occupations, a pattern missed by a single continuous
growth coefficient. For our study, binned or flexible gap specifications are
useful checks for thresholds or asymmetry around equal-status pairs.

## 41. Could occupational growth or decline confound the status gradient?

**The current paper does not claim that the status gradient is independent of
every form of occupational restructuring.**

The fixed effects remove several stable propensities to change, but growth and
decline are time-varying structural conditions. The ongoing OOH analysis is
therefore important: it can test whether content direction aligns with, or
remains distinct from, independently measured occupational growth and labor-
market outcomes.

## 42. Are OOH projections outcomes, expectations, or opportunity signals?

**They are best treated as forward-looking signals of occupational demand,
not as realized worker outcomes.**

Song and colleagues distinguish short-term realized change from long-term
projections and show that they capture different processes. Our complementary
analysis should preserve that distinction and should not describe projected
growth as an observed consequence of skill diffusion.

## 43. Can your findings explain why workers become trapped in declining
occupations?

**They offer a possible content-based mechanism, but they do not establish
that worker-level explanation.**

If expanding occupations increasingly retain socio-cognitive requirements
that are harder to access from declining fields, content recomposition could
strengthen mobility barriers. Testing that claim requires linking skill
profiles to actual origin-destination moves and comparing feasible
alternatives for workers with different education and experience.

# VII. Useful Phrases Under Pressure

- "That distinction is central to the paper."
- "Let me separate the observed pattern from its interpretation."
- "The short answer is no; we do not observe a direct worker flow."
- "That is a limitation of the current design and a natural next step."
- "Our baseline design answers a different but complementary question."
- "Could you please repeat the last part a little more slowly?"
- "If I understand correctly, you are asking whether the relation is observed
  or constructed. Is that right?"
