# Q&A Preparation

## A Matthew Effect in Occupational Skill Content

These answers are written for spoken English at B1 level. The first paragraph
under each question is the main answer. Use the optional extension only when the
questioner asks for more detail.

## A simple structure for every answer

1. **Answer first:** "Yes," "No," or "That is an important limitation."
2. **Give one reason:** explain the relevant result or design choice.
3. **State the limit:** say clearly what the paper does not establish.

Useful opening phrases:

- "Thank you. That is an important question."
- "The short answer is yes, but with one qualification."
- "I agree that this is a central limitation."
- "Our evidence cannot establish that directly. What it does show is..."
- "Let me separate two issues."

# Most Likely Questions

## 1. Why do you call this diffusion if you do not observe direct transmission?

That is an important distinction. We do not claim that skill A moves directly
from one specific occupation to another. We use diffusion to describe a
relational pattern: a skill appears or disappears relative to the occupations
that already hold it. The model tests whether millions of these opportunities
are organized by profile similarity and signed status distance.

**Optional extension:** In the paper, source means a baseline holder that
defines the comparison. It is not a traced sender. A more cautious term would be
directional updating, but diffusion connects our design to gravity models and
relational theories of change.

## 2. Can you make a causal claim about occupational status?

No. The design is observational, so we do not identify a causal effect of
status. Our claim is narrower: signed status gaps strongly organize skill change
after we control for profile similarity, stable endpoint differences, and
skill-specific change.

**Optional extension:** A causal design would require an external change in
occupational status or in access to particular skill pathways. We see this as an
important next step.

## 3. Could common technological shocks produce the same pattern?

A common shock could make many occupations adopt the same skill. But it would
also need to produce opposite directions for cognitive and physical skills
along the same status hierarchy. That is harder to explain with one common
shock. We also use a within-stratum permutation test and separate time periods;
the main directional pattern remains.

**Optional extension:** These checks do not remove every possible correlated
shock. They show that the result is not explained only by different average
rates of change across skill types or source-status groups.

## 4. Is your status measure circular because it includes cognitive content?

This is a valid concern. Our main status index combines wages, education, and
cognitive task content, all measured at baseline. But we repeat the analysis
using wages alone and education alone. The same directional pattern remains.
So the result is not produced only by cognitive content appearing in both the
status measure and the outcome.

## 5. What is genuinely new compared with the job-ad studies?

The novelty is not only the data source. Job-ad studies measure employer demand,
profile change, or skill diversity within jobs and occupations. We estimate a
different object: movement relative to a specific baseline holder, using the
signed status gap between occupational endpoints. This allows us to distinguish
upward and downward paths for different skill types.

**Optional extension:** Tong and colleagues do discuss direction in skill
embedding space. Our distinction is more specific: their direction is not the
signed status relation between a source occupation and a target occupation.

## 6. Why should O*NET represent realized occupational content better than job ads?

O*NET is based on incumbent and analyst assessments of what occupations require.
For that reason, it is closer to established occupational content than an
employer's advertised demand. But "realized" should not be understood as direct
observation inside every workplace. O*NET is still a standardized measurement
system with reporting and updating limits.

## 7. Why is this a Matthew effect rather than simple occupational polarization?

Polarization describes the distribution of cognitive and physical content.
The Matthew effect refers to the process that reproduces this distribution.
Occupations already positioned near cognitive content are more likely to gain
and retain it, while physical content becomes concentrated below. The advantage
therefore accumulates through repeated change.

**Optional extension:** We use the term at the occupation level, not the
individual level. We are describing cumulative advantage among occupational
positions.

## 8. How do you know adoption and abandonment are part of one process?

They are separate outcomes, but their directions reinforce the same divide.
Cognitive skills are adopted more above and abandoned more below. Physical
skills are adopted more below and abandoned more above. If the two processes
were corrective, their directions would offset each other. Empirically, they
compound.

## 9. Why use an RCA threshold instead of raw O*NET scores?

RCA identifies whether a skill is especially important in an occupation
relative to the wider occupational system. This gives us a comparable measure
of skill presence. Because a relative index can create mechanical changes, we
freeze its denominator and also analyze raw importance changes. The central
directional result remains.

## 10. Why does the sign of the status gap matter more than its size?

Two occupations can be equally far apart in status, but movement upward is not
the same as movement downward. An absolute gap removes this information. Our
counterfactual models show that profile distance and unsigned status distance
produce almost flat aggregate gradients. The signed model recovers much more of
the observed pattern.

# Likely Methods Questions

## 11. Why can you not include source and target fixed effects together?

The status gap is target status minus source status. If we include fixed effects
for both endpoints, the gap is fully determined by those fixed effects and
cannot be estimated separately. We therefore use two complementary models: one
with source and skill fixed effects, and one with target and skill fixed
effects. The main pattern appears in both.

## 12. Does having many possible sources count the same adoption event many times?

Yes, one target-skill event can be connected to several eligible baseline
holders. That is part of the relational risk-set design, but it may create
implicit weighting. We therefore estimate a frequency-weighted robustness check
that gives equal total weight to each target-skill event. All slope directions
remain the same, and the magnitudes change only modestly.

## 13. Why use a complementary log-log gravity model?

Adoption and abandonment are rare events observed over a time interval. The
complementary log-log link is appropriate for a discrete-time hazard
interpretation. The gravity structure also lets the event depend on endpoint
propensities, profile distance, and the directional status gap.

## 14. Could occupational reclassification or O*NET updating create false change?

That risk cannot be removed completely. We restrict the analysis to occupations
observed at both endpoints and harmonize the occupational universe. We also test
separate sub-periods and alternative outcome definitions. The directional
pattern is not limited to one update period.

## 15. How sensitive are the results to the skill taxonomy?

The main contrast is robust to moderate changes in classification. We use
established work on skill polarization and nestedness, and we also test
misclassification in the backup analysis. The exact coefficients change, but
the cognitive-up and physical-down pattern remains.

## 16. Why combine wages, education, and cognitive content into one status index?

Occupational status is multidimensional. Wages capture economic rewards,
education captures credential requirements, and cognitive content captures the
vertical organization of work. The first principal component summarizes their
shared hierarchy. Importantly, using each component separately produces the
same basic asymmetry.

# Theory and Interpretation Questions

## 17. Which mechanism is most important: closure, emulation, or absorptive capacity?

Our design cannot separate these mechanisms. They all predict that similar
occupations can have different paths because of their relative status. The
paper identifies this shared directional signature. Distinguishing mechanisms
would require firm-level data, institutional variation, or direct evidence on
who controls task allocation and credential boundaries.

## 18. Could lower-status occupations be gaining cognitive skills in absolute terms?

Yes. Our result does not mean that lower-status occupations never gain cognitive
skills. It is a relational statement: cognitive adoption is more likely toward
higher-status targets relative to baseline holders, and cognitive abandonment
is more likely below. General upgrading and unequal directional concentration
can happen at the same time.

## 19. Does this describe occupations or workers?

It describes occupations. We do not observe individual workers learning or
losing skills, and we do not observe worker mobility between occupations.
The implication for workers is therefore indirect: changes in occupational
requirements may shape their opportunities, but that link needs separate data.

## 20. Is the AI argument too speculative for these data?

The paper does not estimate AI exposure or displacement. We present AI as an
implication, not as a tested mechanism. The evidence shows unequal directional
capacity for skill change. If new technologies reward cognitive complements,
this structure may create an additional barrier for occupations concentrated in
physical content.

## 21. Why should the result generalize outside the United States?

We should not assume that the magnitude generalizes. Credential systems,
unions, training institutions, and wage-setting may change the strength of the
pattern. The theoretical process may travel, but cross-national comparison is
needed to test whether institutions weaken or strengthen directional sorting.

## 22. What would falsify your argument?

The argument would be weakened if signed status gaps did not predict change
after accounting for profile similarity and endpoint propensities. It would
also be weakened if adoption and abandonment moved in opposite, offsetting
directions, or if direction-blind models reproduced the aggregate gradients as
well as the directional model. We do not observe those patterns.

# Questions About Magnitude and Meaning

## 23. Are the effects substantively large, or only statistically significant because you have 40 million observations?

The large sample makes small coefficients precise, so statistical significance
is not enough. Our main substantive test is the aggregate projection. The
directional model recovers most of the cognitive status gradient and part of the
physical gradient, while direction-blind models remain close to flat. This
shows that direction has meaningful aggregate consequences, although the model
does not explain the full observed pattern.

## 24. Why does the model explain less of the physical gradient?

The signed status channel is only one part of occupational change. The physical
gradient may also depend on sector, technology, organizational restructuring,
and institutions that are not fully represented by profile distance and status.
We interpret the projection as a test of directional sufficiency, not as a
complete prediction model.

## 25. What is the main contribution in one sentence?

We show that occupational skill change is a status-asymmetric relational
process: cognitive skills move upward, physical skills move downward, and
adoption and abandonment together reproduce occupational inequality.

# When You Do Not Know the Answer

Use one of these responses. Do not improvise a result that is not in the paper.

- "That is a very good question. We do not test it directly in this paper."
- "I do not have the exact number now, but the direction is stable across the
  robustness checks."
- "I would prefer to check the result before giving you a precise answer."
- "Our data cannot distinguish those mechanisms, so I see that as a next step."
- "I agree that this interpretation should remain cautious."
