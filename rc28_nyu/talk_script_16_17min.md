# A Matthew Effect in Occupational Skill Content

## Title Slide - 0:00-0:45

Good morning, and thank you for being here.

Before I begin, English is not my first language, so I may speak a little more
slowly. Thank you for your patience, and I will be grateful for your comments
during the discussion.

The main finding is simple. Socio-cognitive content becomes increasingly
concentrated in higher-status occupations, while sensory-physical content
becomes increasingly concentrated in lower-status occupations. We describe
this as a Matthew effect in occupational skill content.

## Occupations Are in Constant Transformation - 0:45-1:15

Occupational titles may remain stable while their content changes. The tasks
and skill requirements attached to an occupation can be added, recombined,
retained, or abandoned.

So the question is not only whether occupations grow or decline. It is how
their content is recomposed over time.

## Technological Change Raises the Pressure to Update - 1:15-1:45

Technological change has increased the value of cognitive and analytical
capabilities. AI has renewed the expectation that many occupations will
require more problem-solving content.

If these valued skills spread broadly, technology may open new pathways. But
if they are absorbed mainly at the top, the same pressure can widen existing
divides.

## Upskilling Pressure May Be Strongest at the Bottom - 1:45-2:20

This is not an obvious result, because the pressure to update may be strongest
near the bottom of the occupational hierarchy.

Lower-status occupations often face greater risk of replacement. Job-posting
evidence also suggests that low-wage jobs are diversifying their skill
requirements.

But pressure is not the same as realized change. Job ads can demand more
skills, but they do not tell us which skills become established and retained.
That is the gap this paper addresses.

## Heterogeneity Depends on Skill Type and Specialization - 2:20-2:50

Skill change does not occur in a neutral skill space. Socio-cognitive skills
are associated with higher education, higher wages, and more advantaged
occupations. Sensory-physical skills are more concentrated in lower-status
work.

Cognitive skills also differ in specificity: some are general, while others
are more specialized. So we ask whether these skill types follow different
paths through the occupational hierarchy.

# I. Skill Diffusion - 2:50-3:00

The core idea is this: relatedness connects occupations, but status directs the
movement.

## Skill Diffusion Is Directional Updating - 3:00-3:45

What do we observe? Occupations gain and lose skill requirements over time.

What makes this relational is that each changing skill is already held by a
set of other occupations. Those current holders locate the target in a field
of occupational positions.

We call this pattern diffusion: the changing distribution of skill
requirements across occupations, traced relative to the occupations that
already hold each skill. This is directional updating across occupational
profiles, rather than a claim about direct transmission between a particular
pair.

## Proximity Facilitates Diffusion - 3:45-4:15

The first force is proximity. If two occupations have similar task profiles, a
skill held by one is more plausible for the other.

Think of nurses and doctors. Their task profiles are relatively close.

This proximity is symmetric: the distance from nurses to doctors is the same
as the distance from doctors to nurses. This is the standard gravity logic.

## Status Governs Diffusion Direction - 4:15-4:55

But proximity alone has no direction. Nurses and doctors have one symmetric
distance, but the two orderings are not equivalent.

A skill moving from doctors toward nurses moves down the status hierarchy. A
skill moving from nurses toward doctors moves up.

The same pair can therefore be close in task space, while its signed status gap
changes when we reverse source and target. Our argument is that occupational
skill diffusion is status-sorted and asymmetric.

## We Ask Two Descriptive Questions - 4:55-5:25

The paper asks two descriptive questions.

First, is skill updating symmetric with respect to occupational status, or is
it status-directed?

Second, if it is directed, does it move upward or downward, and does the answer
differ by skill type?

We do not identify one unique mechanism. We establish the directional pattern.

# II. Data and Research Design - 5:25-5:35

I now turn to the data and design.

## Data - 5:35-6:05

We use O*NET skill profiles from 2015 to 2024. The analysis covers 741
occupations and 160 skill requirements. Skill specialization is defined with
the standard RCA threshold.

Occupational status is measured at baseline using median wages, required
education, and cognitive task content. These 2015 measures are held fixed
before subsequent occupational change.

## Dependent Variables - 6:05-6:45

There are two dependent variables: adoption and abandonment.

In adoption, the target gains a skill that current holders already have. In
abandonment, the target sheds a skill that current holders retain.

The target is the occupation that changes. The source is a positional reference
point that locates the target relative to current holders.

This produces about 21.5 million adoption opportunities and 18.6 million
abandonment opportunities.

## Three Skill Classes Capture Function and Specificity - 6:45-7:15

We classify the 160 requirements into three groups: general socio-cognitive,
specialized socio-cognitive, and sensory-physical skills.

The first two groups differ in cognitive specificity. The third includes
physical, sensory, and manual requirements.

This classification allows us to test whether different kinds of skills move
differently through the occupational hierarchy.

## An Asymmetric Gravity Model for Skill Diffusion - 7:15-8:00

The model is a gravity model for directed skill opportunities.

Updating depends on occupational characteristics and on the separation between
occupations.

We divide separation into two parts. Profile distance is symmetric and captures
how similar two occupational skill profiles are. The signed status gap is
directional: target status minus source status.

If the target is above the source, the gap is positive. If the target is below
the source, it is negative. The model asks whether this direction matters after
accounting for ordinary task proximity.

## Estimation - 8:00-8:40

The parameter of interest is beta, the coefficient on the signed status gap.
A positive beta orients updating toward higher-status targets. A negative beta
orients it toward lower-status targets.

We estimate separate coefficients for adoption and abandonment within each
skill class.

We use two complementary fixed-effect specifications. Panel A holds source and
skill fixed. Panel B holds target and skill fixed. Together, they help separate
the signed status gap from stable characteristics of occupations and skills.

# III. Findings - 8:40-8:50

I now move to the findings.

## Panel A: Skill Diffusion Is Directed Around Fixed Sources - 8:50-10:50

Panel A holds the source and the skill fixed. So we compare targets that sit
below or above the same source, for the same skill.

On the horizontal axis, negative values mean the target is below the source.
Positive values mean the target is above the source. The vertical axis shows
the relative hazard compared with an equal-status pair.

Let me begin with adoption, in the left panel.

Socio-cognitive skills are taken up more readily when the adopting occupation
ranks above the occupation that already holds the skill. In other words,
cognitive skills move up the status gap.

Sensory-physical skills show the mirror pattern. They are taken up more readily
when the adopting occupation ranks below the current holder. So physical skills
move down the status gap.

Now look at abandonment, in the right panel. The key question is whether skill
loss offsets this divergence or amplifies it.

If abandonment corrected the adoption pattern, cognitive skills would be shed
at the top or retained at the bottom. But that is not what happens.

Socio-cognitive skills are more likely to be shed when the occupation dropping
them ranks below the occupation that still holds them. Sensory-physical skills
show the opposite: they are more likely to be dropped from above.

Adoption and abandonment are therefore not separate stories. They are two arms
of the same process. Cognitive content is gained and retained upward. Physical
content is gained and retained downward.

## Panel B: The Same Diffusion Pattern Holds Around Fixed Targets - 10:50-11:40

Panel B provides a complementary check. Could the pattern in Panel A simply
reflect that some target occupations are generally more likely to adopt or
abandon skills?

Here we hold the target and the skill fixed. The target is the same. The skill
is the same. We compare sources located above and below that target.

The signs remain the same. This shows that the pattern is not driven only by
the target's general tendency to change. It reflects the signed status gap
between source and target.

## The Pattern Cannot Be Reduced To... - 11:40-12:10

The complementary fixed effects absorb stable characteristics of occupations,
including size, centrality, and their general tendency to change.

Skill fixed effects also absorb stable differences in prevalence and
diffusibility across skills.

The same domain reversal remains in both specifications. So the result is not
driven simply by which occupations change more or which skills diffuse more
easily.

## The Signed-Gap Model Recovers the Status Gradient - 12:10-13:00

The next question is whether the relational estimates recover the broader
occupational gradient.

The black line is the observed gradient. The blue line is the directional
status-gap model. The orange line removes the direction of the gap, and the
gray line retains only profile distance.

The directional model tracks the observed gradient much more closely. The
direction-blind alternatives are much flatter.

This is not a prediction exercise with new data. The comparison shows that
proximity and gap size are not enough. The sign of the status gap is the
informative component.

# IV. Discussion - 13:00-13:10

Let me close with the takeaways, implications, and limits.

## Takeaways - 13:10-14:15

The paper has three takeaways.

First, occupational content changes through adoption and abandonment. These
two margins do not offset one another. They reinforce the same pattern.

Second, skill diffusion is status-directed, but its direction differs by skill
type. Socio-cognitive diffusion is oriented upward, while sensory-physical
diffusion is oriented downward.

Third, the pattern remains when cognitive content is removed from the baseline
status measure. So the result is not built into how we measure occupational
status.

Taken together, the findings reveal a Matthew-effect pattern. Occupational
updating reinforces the cognitive-physical divide already embedded in the
occupational hierarchy.

External validation is still ongoing. Preliminary ESCO results provide partial
support using independently constructed occupational-skill data. We are also
using the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook to compare the pattern with
independently measured labor-market outcomes.

## Implications - 14:15-14:50

The implication for debates on technology and AI is direct. Technological
pressure may raise demand for cognitive capabilities without redistributing
them evenly across occupations.

Lower-status occupations may face greater pressure to adapt, but weaker
incorporation and retention of socio-cognitive content.

Inequality can therefore be reproduced inside occupations, before workers move
or jobs disappear.

## Limitations - 14:50-15:30

There are three limits.

First, this is an occupation-level analysis. We do not observe the decisions
behind each skill change.

Second, the design is observational. We estimate directional associations, not
direct pairwise transmission or one unique causal mechanism.

Third, the evidence comes from U.S. data. Credentialing, wage-setting, unions,
and training systems may moderate the pattern elsewhere.

These limits define the scope of the claim.

## Thank You - 15:30-16:15

Let me end with the central point.

The content of occupational change is not evenly shared across the hierarchy.

Socio-cognitive content becomes concentrated upward. Sensory-physical content
becomes concentrated downward. This happens through both gains and losses of
occupational requirements.

Even when pressure to upskill is widespread, realized updating can reinforce
the occupational hierarchy.

Thank you.
