# A Matthew Effect in Occupational Skill Content

## 15-minute talk script — clear spoken English (B1)

**Target:** about 1,550 spoken words.  
**Delivery:** speak slowly, pause after each main result, and do not explain every
detail shown on the slides.

### Title slide — 0:00

Good morning. Thank you for being here.

This paper is about how the skill content of occupations changes over time.

We ask a simple question: does this change reduce occupational inequality, or
does it reproduce it?

Our answer is that change is strongly directional. Socio-cognitive skills tend
to move upward in the occupational hierarchy. Sensory and physical skills tend
to move downward.

We call this a Matthew effect in occupational skill content.

### The Puzzle — 0:35

Let me begin with the puzzle.

One common expectation is that new technology spreads cognitive requirements
across many occupations. Recent studies of job ads also show strong pressure to
upskill at the bottom of the occupational hierarchy.

This could suggest convergence.

But we find a different pattern. Cognitive skills diffuse upward, while
physical skills diffuse downward. So skill change can increase the divide,
rather than reduce it.

### From Upskilling Pressure to Realized Change — 1:10

The key distinction is between pressure to change and realized change.

An employer may request new skills in a job ad. But this does not mean that the
skill becomes an established part of the occupation.

Also, occupations do not change alone. They are connected through similar
tasks, training, and productive relations.

So we ask: which skills move, and do they move toward higher- or lower-status
occupations?

### Two Different Objects — 1:45

This question differs from recent job-ad studies.

Those studies measure employer demand. Tong and colleagues study change in an
occupation's advertised skill profile. Han and Cheng study skill diversity
inside jobs.

These are important contributions. But they do not estimate movement relative
to a specific source occupation and its status.

We use O*NET to study occupational skill profiles. Our main object is the
signed status gap between two occupations in a possible diffusion event.

### Demand Signals and Realized Flows — 2:20

This figure summarizes the difference.

On the left, the question is how the demanded skill profile changes within one
occupation.

On the right, our question is relational. Given two occupations, does a skill
move toward the occupation above or below in status?

We do not observe a direct transfer from one occupation to another. We observe
many structured opportunities for diffusion, and test whether they follow a
systematic direction.

### The Architecture We Build Upon — 2:55

We build on two ideas.

First, skills are polarized into a socio-cognitive domain and a
sensory-physical domain.

Second, skills have a nested structure. Some skills are broad and support many
other skills. Others are more specialized.

Together, these ideas give us three classes: general socio-cognitive,
specialized socio-cognitive, and sensory-physical skills.

### Section I: Directional Skill Diffusion — 3:20

I will now explain our theoretical argument.

### Two Elementary Events — 3:25

We study two events: adoption and abandonment.

In adoption, one occupation holds a skill and another does not. We observe
whether the second occupation later adopts it.

In abandonment, both occupations hold the skill at the start. We observe
whether the target occupation later drops it.

For both events, we examine profile distance, the signed status gap, and the
type of skill.

### Theory: Directed Assortative Mixing — 4:00

We expect diffusion to be more likely between occupations with similar skill
profiles. This is the assortative part of the argument.

But similarity has no direction. A nurse and a physician are close in tasks
and training. Still, a nurse adopting a skill held by physicians is not the
same event as a physician adopting a skill held by nurses.

Our claim is that status gives direction to this relationship.

We expect socio-cognitive skills to move upward and sensory-physical skills to
move downward. Adoption and abandonment may then reinforce the same divide.

### Mechanisms Behind the Gap — 4:40

Several mechanisms may produce this pattern: emulation, task allocation,
absorptive capacity, credentials, and different valuation of similar work.

We do not identify these mechanisms separately. They lead to one shared
prediction: even among related occupations, relative status should predict the
direction of skill change.

### Section II: Data and Methods — 5:05

I will now describe the empirical design.

### Data — 5:10

We combine O*NET data from 2015 and 2024 with wages, education, and cognitive
task content.

We study 741 occupations and 160 skill requirements. This produces about 40
million directed diffusion opportunities: 21.5 million for adoption and 18.6
million for abandonment.

### Diffusion Events — 5:40

Our unit is a directed triple: a source occupation, a target occupation, and a
skill.

We define skill presence with a revealed comparative advantage threshold.

Most important, the status gap equals target status minus source status. A
positive gap means that the target is above the source. A negative gap means
that the target is below.

This sign is the central information in our design.

### Identification Strategy — 6:15

We control for baseline skill-profile distance, because similar occupations
are naturally more connected.

Skill fixed effects absorb differences in how easily each skill changes. We
also use complementary source and target fixed-effect strategies.

Finally, we cluster uncertainty by source, target, and skill.

The goal is to test whether direction matters beyond similarity and stable
occupation or skill differences.

### Skill Classes — 6:45

Here you can see the three skill classes.

We have 49 general socio-cognitive skills, 48 specialized socio-cognitive
skills, and 63 sensory-physical skills.

This allows us to compare not only cognitive and physical content, but also
broad and specialized cognitive skills.

### Directional Gravity Model — 7:10

This is the model. I will not go through the full equation.

The important point is that we split the status gap into an upward part and a
downward part. A model using only absolute distance knows how far apart two
occupations are, but not which one is above.

We estimate the model separately for adoption, abandonment, and each skill
class.

### Section III: Descriptive Patterns — 7:40

Let us first look at the descriptive evidence.

### Adoption and Abandonment Gradients — 7:45

The first pattern is expected. Profile distance matters.

Adoption becomes less likely when occupations are less similar. Abandonment
becomes more likely as their profiles move apart.

So similarity defines where change is possible. But it does not tell us the
direction.

### Status-Gap Gradients and Networks — 8:15

Here the directional pattern appears.

Socio-cognitive adoption becomes more likely toward higher-status targets.
Sensory-physical adoption becomes more likely toward lower-status targets.

Abandonment reinforces this pattern: cognitive content is more often lost
below, while physical content is more often lost above.

### Descriptive Result — 8:50

The descriptive result is simple.

Profile similarity tells us where skills can move. Relative status tells us in
which direction they are more likely to move.

Now we ask whether this survives the full model.

### Section IV: Gravity Model Results — 9:10

The answer is yes.

### Fixed-Effects Gravity Estimates — 9:15

Please focus on the signs and their consistency.

For socio-cognitive skills, adoption points upward and abandonment removes
content below.

For sensory-physical skills, adoption points downward and abandonment removes
content above.

The two events work together, and the pattern appears in both fixed-effect
strategies.

### What the Model Shows — 9:55

The model gives us three conclusions.

First, direction matters beyond profile similarity.

Second, the result is not explained only by dynamic source occupations,
receptive target occupations, or skills that change easily.

Third, adoption and abandonment reinforce each other. They do not cancel out.

### From Dyads to Occupational Stratification I — 10:25

We then aggregate the predicted events across occupational status.

For socio-cognitive skills, the directional model reproduces the upward pattern
in the observed data.

The comparison with the null models is important. A distance-only model is
almost flat. A model that ignores the sign of status also misses the pattern.

### From Dyads to Occupational Stratification II — 10:55

For sensory-physical skills, we see the mirror image. The observed pattern
slopes toward the bottom, and the directional model follows it.

This connects small dyadic events to the larger occupational hierarchy.

### Threats to Inference — 11:20

We test four main threats: a mechanical index effect, stable occupation and
skill differences, alternative measurement choices, and the influence of one
special time period.

The size of the estimates changes, but the directional asymmetry remains. The
full checks are in the backup slides.

### Why This Matters — 11:50

This matters because occupations are not fixed bundles of tasks. Their content
changes, and this change is organized by status.

Cognitive content becomes more concentrated in higher-status occupations.
Physical content becomes more concentrated below.

Models without direction cannot explain these two gradients.

### Implications: AI and a Compounding Barrier — 12:20

This has an important implication for technological change and AI.

Workers in physical-intensive occupations may face two barriers. Their current
tasks may be more exposed to substitution, and the pathways toward cognitive
skills may also be status-sorted.

We do not estimate AI displacement directly. Our point is about unequal
capacity to adapt: exposure and access to new skill destinations may be
unequally distributed.

### Limitations — 12:55

There are important limits.

Our analysis is at the occupation level, not the worker or firm level. The
design is observational. The data come only from the United States. And we do
not identify which mechanism produces the pattern.

These limits define the next research questions. They do not remove the strong
directional regularity in the data.

### Conclusion — 13:25

Let me close with three points.

First, skill diffusion is not a general process of cognitive upgrading. It is
sorted by occupational status.

Second, adoption and abandonment work together. Socio-cognitive skills move
upward, while sensory-physical skills move downward.

Third, these small directional differences accumulate. Occupations already
rich in cognitive content are better positioned to gain more, while physical
content remains concentrated below.

So occupations change, but the direction of change is not neutral. It can
reproduce inequality inside the content of occupations themselves.

Thank you.

### Thank You — 14:20

I look forward to your questions.

## Emergency short ending

Use this only if the chair signals that you have less than 30 seconds:

To conclude, occupational skill change is directional. Cognitive skills move
up, physical skills move down, and adoption and abandonment reinforce each
other. This is how local skill changes can reproduce occupational inequality.
